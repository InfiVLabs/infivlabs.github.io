How long does it take to form a habit?

How long it takes to form a habit, from the research: a median of 66 days in the UCL study, from 18 to 254 days, and where the 21-day rule came from.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

It takes about two months on average, and it varies a lot from person to person. In the best-known study, by Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London (2010), a new daily behavior took a median of 66 days to become as automatic as it was going to get. Individual times ranged from 18 to 254 days. The popular "21 days" figure does not come from habit research at all.

What the 66-day study measured

The study, published in the European Journal of Social Psychology, recruited 96 volunteers. Each picked one new eating, drinking or exercise behavior to do once a day in the same situation for 12 weeks. Examples included eating a piece of fruit with lunch, a 15-minute run before dinner and 50 sit-ups after morning coffee.

Every day, participants logged whether they had done it and rated how automatic it felt, with statements such as "I do it automatically" and "I do it without thinking." The researchers then modeled how that automaticity score rose over time for each person.

The pattern was a curve, not a switch. Automaticity rose quickly in the first weeks, then flattened out. The point where it reached 95% of its plateau is the "habit formed" moment behind the 66-day figure.

Some details are often left out:

  • The model fitted well for 39 people, so the 66 days describes them rather than all 96.
  • The study lasted 84 days. The 254-day figure is a projection of where one person's curve was heading, not something anyone was observed doing.
  • By the end, about half the participants had not reached a habit-level score.
  • Exercise habits took about one and a half times as long as eating or drinking habits.
  • Missing one day did not materially affect habit formation. Missing many days did lower how automatic the behavior became.

What later research adds

A 2024 systematic review in the journal Healthcare (Singh and colleagues, University of South Australia) pooled studies of health habits. Only four reported how long habit formation took. Their medians ranged from 59 to 66 days, their means from 106 to 154 days, and individual people from 4 to 335 days. The authors' summary: habits can start forming within about two months, and the time varies widely between people and behaviors.

The mean is higher than the median because a few people take a very long time, which pulls the average up. For planning, "two months for many people, longer for some" is the honest reading.

Where the 21-day rule came from

In 1960 the plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz wrote in the preface of his book Psycho-Cybernetics that it "requires a minimum of about 21 days" for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to form. He based this on watching patients get used to a new face, and on people adjusting after losing a limb or moving house. It was an observation about adjusting to change, it said "minimum", and it was not a study of habits.

Over the years, "a minimum of about 21 days" became "21 days to form a habit." It is a fine length for a first challenge. It is not a finish line.

Practical rules that follow from the research:

  1. Tie the habit to a cue that already happens every day: after coffee, after brushing your teeth, when you sit down for lunch. The study's participants all chose a fixed situation.
  2. Keep it small at first. Simple behaviors became automatic faster.
  3. Repeat it consistently. Participants who repeated the behavior more consistently followed the habit curve more closely.
  4. Don't restart after one miss. One missed day did not matter. Carry on the next day.
  5. Plan for at least two months, and check how automatic the habit feels rather than counting days.

How to tell if a habit has formed

Ask yourself questions like the ones researchers use:

  • Do I do it without having to remember?
  • Do I start before I've really decided to?
  • Would it feel odd to skip it?

If you answer yes to all three, the habit is doing its own work. If not, keep the cue and the small version going.

Do habit trackers help?

A tracker measures repetitions, not automaticity, so the number of filled squares is not proof that a habit has formed. What it does well is make consistency visible, and consistency is the part of the process you control. Seeing a missed day also helps you avoid missing a second one. Questions

Is the 21-day rule true?

No. It comes from a 1960 self-help book by a plastic surgeon describing how long people took to adjust to changes, and he called it a minimum. Measured habit formation took a median of 66 days in the UCL study.

Does missing a day ruin a habit?

No. In the UCL study, missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Missing many days did make the behavior less automatic in the end.

Why do some habits take longer than others?

Effort and complexity matter. In the UCL study, exercise habits took about one and a half times as long as eating or drinking habits to reach their plateau.

Is 66 days the same for everyone?

No. Individual times in that study ranged from 18 days to a projected 254 days, and a 2024 review found a range of 4 to 335 days across studies.
