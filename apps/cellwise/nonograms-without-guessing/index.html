Nonograms without guessing: is every nonogram solvable by logic?

Can every nonogram be solved without guessing? How unique solutions work, why some puzzles force a guess, and how to tell when you have only missed a step.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

No. A nonogram can be finished by pure logic only when its clues allow exactly one picture and some chain of reasoning reaches it. Plenty of puzzles in books, newspapers and apps fail that test: some have two or more valid pictures, so at some point you must pick one. Others have a single answer but need such deep reasoning that most people end up guessing anyway. A well-made nonogram is checked for both problems before anyone sees it.

Three kinds of nonogram

KindWhat it feels likeCan you finish without guessing?
Line-solvableEvery step comes from one row or one column at a timeYes, always
Unique but deepYou reach a point where no single line gives anything, but the picture is still fixedYes, with "what if" reasoning across several lines
More than one solutionTwo pictures fit every clueNo. You have to choose, and an app that stores one picture may call the other one wrong

Most good puzzles are line-solvable. That is the standard to look for.

The smallest puzzle that forces a guess

Take a 2×2 grid where every row clue is 1 and every column clue is 1.

11
1■×
1×■

That fits. So does the mirror image:

11
1×■
1■×

The clues cannot tell the two apart. The same trap hides inside larger grids: whenever four undecided squares sit at the corners of a rectangle, and both diagonal patterns satisfy every clue they touch, the puzzle has two answers. If you are ever stuck with exactly such a pattern left and every other square is proved, the puzzle needed a guess, not you.

Why some published puzzles need guessing

Checking a nonogram by hand is slow, and checking it in general is hard even for computers. Deciding whether a nonogram has any solution at all is an NP-complete problem, a result first shown by the Japanese researchers Ueda and Nagao in 1996. Their work also showed that asking "is there another solution?" is just as hard in general. In practice, small and medium grids are easy for a program to check, but someone has to run that check. Puzzles converted straight from a picture, without it, often end with an ambiguous patch: two shapes of a cat's ear, or a cloud edge that could sit one square higher.

Logic that looks like guessing

There is a technique that feels like guessing but is not: reasoning by contradiction.

  1. Pick an undecided square and suppose it is filled.
  2. Follow what that forces in its row and column, and in the lines those changes touch.
  3. If you reach a line whose clue can no longer fit, the supposition was impossible, so the square is empty.
  4. If nothing breaks, you have learned nothing yet. Undo every mark from the test and try another square.

The difference from guessing is step 4. A guess keeps the marks and hopes; a contradiction test only keeps a result it has proved. Use a pencil or a different color for the test marks so you can erase them cleanly. You only need this on "unique but deep" puzzles. On line-solvable ones, it is always a sign that a simpler step was missed.

Stuck, or a puzzle that needs a guess?

Before you blame the puzzle, run this check:

  • Re-read every clue in every line that changed in your last few moves. The missed step is nearly always there.
  • Check the edges. A filled square in the first or second position often fixes the whole first run.
  • Look for runs that cannot reach a square. If no run can stretch to a square, it is empty.
  • Recount each line's slack (length minus clue total minus gaps). Lines with small slack give squares.
  • Look at finished runs. A run that already has its full length needs an X on both ends.

If all of that gives nothing, and what is left is a rectangle pattern like the 2×2 above, the puzzle has more than one solution. On paper, pick either and enjoy the picture. More techniques for getting unstuck are in nonogram tips for hard puzzles, and the basics are in how to solve nonograms.

How puzzle makers check for a unique solution

A careful maker runs each puzzle through a solver that only uses the deductions a person can make line by line. If the solver fills the whole grid, the puzzle is line-solvable and therefore has one solution. If it stops partway, the maker changes a few squares of the picture and runs it again, or throws the puzzle away.

How Cellwise handles it

Cellwise runs a line solver over every one of its 1,144 puzzles and rejects any grid the solver cannot finish or that has a second solution. The endless mode does the same on your phone: it builds a mosaic, tests it, changes up to three squares if the solver stalls, and discards it if that fails. A hint in the app shows a square the clues already force. For "what if" tests, the Pencil tool marks squares as tentative, then lets you keep them or undo them all at once.

Questions

Is every nonogram solvable?

No. Some nonograms have more than one picture that fits the clues, and a few have none because of a printing error. A puzzle checked by a solver has exactly one.

Do nonograms have a unique solution?

Well-made ones do. Uniqueness is a property the puzzle maker has to check; it does not come automatically from turning a picture into clues.

Should you ever guess in a nonogram?

Only on a puzzle that truly has two answers. On a unique puzzle, a "what if" test you are willing to undo is logic; a guess you keep is a gamble that can ruin the grid.

Why does my nonogram app say a correct-looking answer is wrong?

If the puzzle has two solutions, the app may only accept the picture it stored. Your grid fits the clues, but the puzzle itself was ambiguous.
