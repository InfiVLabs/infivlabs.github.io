Choosing a nonogram app: 10 things to test before you commit

Choosing a nonogram app for Android or iPhone? Ten things to test in your first ten minutes, from unique solutions and 20×20 controls to ads and prices.

Updated 26 Nov 20264 min read

The right nonogram app is the one whose puzzles never need a guess, whose controls stay accurate on a 20×20 grid, and whose ads and lives you can live with. Store ratings will not tell you those things, but ten minutes of play will. Here is what to test, and what a good answer looks like.

The puzzles

1. Do the puzzles have one solution? Read the store description: does it say puzzles are checked for a unique, logic-only solution? Then play a few large ones. If you keep reaching a point where two arrangements fit, the puzzles were not checked. Our guide to nonograms without guessing explains how to spot that.

2. Is there a range of sizes? You want small grids to warm up and 15×15 or 20×20 grids for longer sessions. Check whether the big ones are in the free part of the app or behind a purchase.

3. Are there color nonograms? Color puzzles use slightly different rules (runs of different colors may touch) and make a nice change from black and white. Not every app has them.

4. Is there a daily puzzle? A daily grid gives you a reason to open the app for five minutes, and a calendar lets you catch up on missed days. See how many past days are free.

The controls

5. Can you fill a whole run by dragging? On a phone, tapping 15 squares one by one is slow and error-prone. Look for drag-to-fill with a counter that shows how many squares you have covered.

6. Does zoom keep the clues in view? On a 20×20 grid you will zoom in. In a good app, the row and column clues stay pinned to the screen edge while you scroll; in a weak one, you lose them and have to zoom back out to read each clue.

7. Do finished clues cross themselves off? Automatic strike-through on completed lines saves a lot of re-counting and shows where the remaining work is.

8. What happens when you tap the wrong square? Some apps mark mistakes at once, some take a life, some stay quiet like paper. Decide which you prefer. If you dislike losing lives over a mis-tap, look for a mode without them and check what it costs.

The money

9. How many ads, and when? Play three or four puzzles in a row. Count interstitial ads (full-screen ads between puzzles), check whether there is a banner during play, and see whether hints or extra lives are offered for watching an ad.

10. What does paying change, and how? Look for a one-time purchase versus a subscription, and read exactly what it removes: ads only, or also limits on hints, lives, puzzle packs and the daily archive. Check the price in the store listing's in-app purchase section before you start a free trial.

A ten-minute test plan

  1. Solve the first easy puzzle and note how many taps it takes to fill a long run.
  2. Open the largest grid you can reach, zoom in and check that the clues stay visible.
  3. Make a deliberate mistake and see what happens.
  4. Ask for one hint: does it explain a step or just fill a square?
  5. Solve two more puzzles and count the ads you see.
  6. Open the store page and read the purchase options and their prices.
  7. Turn on airplane mode and try to start a puzzle, if you play on the move.

If an app passes the first six steps, it is worth your time. The last one matters only if you play without a signal.

Questions

Is there a nonogram app with no ads?

Most free nonogram apps show ads and sell a purchase that removes them. Before paying, check whether the ad removal is a one-time purchase or a subscription, and whether it also lifts limits on hints and lives.

Are nonogram apps free?

Most are free to download, paid for by ads and optional purchases. The question is what the free part includes: how many puzzles, which sizes, and how many hints.

Can you play nonograms offline?

Many apps work offline once installed, because the puzzles are stored on the phone. Test it in airplane mode. Daily puzzles and cloud saves sometimes need a connection.

What should a good nonogram app have?

Puzzles with one logic-only solution, drag-to-fill, zoom that keeps clues visible, clue strike-through, a choice of how mistakes are handled, and a clear price.
