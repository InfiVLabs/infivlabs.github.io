How to solve rope untangle puzzles

How to solve rope untangle puzzles: the rules, a worked example, eight tips for clearing crossings, and what to do when no single move helps.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

To solve a rope untangle puzzle, move one rope end at a time to an empty peg until no two ropes cross. Start with the rope that has the most crossings, prefer short ropes between neighboring pegs, and use the empty pegs on the outer edge of the board. When no move reduces the count, make a move that frees a peg instead, even if it looks like a step back.

The rules of the rope untangle game

Most rope untangle games share the same core:

  • Each rope joins two pegs. Pegs hold one rope end at most.
  • Some pegs are empty. Those are the only places you can move an end to.
  • You move one end per move. The other end stays where it is.
  • The level is solved when no rope crosses another.

There is a second family of rope games, usually in 3D, where ropes lie in a stack and you must lift the top one first. The tips below are for crossing puzzles, where only the layout matters, not the order of layers.

This family of puzzles goes back to Planarity, a 2005 browser game in which you drag dots instead of rope ends until no lines cross. Rope games keep the goal and change the move: instead of moving a point that drags several lines, you move one end of one rope.

Picture every rope as a straight line

Two ropes cross when their straight lines cross. That makes the board easy to reason about if you stop looking at color and texture and see only lines.

A useful special case: on a board with two rows of pegs, take any two ropes that both run from the top row to the bottom row. They cross exactly when their order flips, meaning one starts to the left of the other on top but ends to the right of it at the bottom.

A worked example

Picture two rows of four pegs. Top row, left to right: 1, 2, 3, 4. Bottom row: 5, 6, 7, 8.

RopeTop pegBottom peg
Red17
Blue25
Cream36

Pegs 4 and 8 are empty. Red runs from the far left on top to the third peg below, so it flips order with both blue and cream. That is two crossings, both on red. Blue and cream keep their order (blue is left of cream on both rows), so they do not cross.

Red is the busiest rope, so move one of its ends. Its top end can go to peg 4: red now runs from 4 to 7, to the right of blue and cream on both rows. Zero crossings, solved in one move. Moving blue or cream first would have taken at least two.

Eight tips for clearing crossings

  1. Find the busiest rope. Count crossings per rope before moving. Fixing the rope with three crossings beats fixing two ropes with one each.
  2. Check both ends. Each rope can move from either end, and the two options often give very different results.
  3. Use the outer ring. A short rope between two neighboring pegs on the outside edge of the board cannot be crossed by anything, because no other straight rope can pass outside it.
  4. Keep ropes short. Long diagonal ropes cut across the board and pick up crossings. Short ropes between neighbors stay out of the way.
  5. Build from one side. Clear one corner fully, then work across. A finished region gives you a stable edge to place the next rope against.
  6. Look for parallel order. Ropes that run side by side without swapping order never cross. Lining ropes up in parallel bands is often the shape of the answer.
  7. Preview before you drop. If the game shows what a move would cross, test two or three target pegs before committing.
  8. Watch the empty pegs. The position of the free pegs decides what is possible. Sometimes the best move is the one that leaves a free peg in a useful place.

When no single move helps

Hard knots are often built so that taking the move that clears the most crossings each time leads to a dead end. Somewhere you have to make a move that clears nothing, or even adds a crossing, to free the peg the next move needs. Signs you are there:

  • Every candidate move leaves the count the same or worse.
  • The ropes you need to move have no empty peg on the useful side.

What to do:

  1. Pick the rope that is in the way of the busiest rope's best target, and move it somewhere harmless, even if one crossing appears.
  2. Now move the busiest rope into the peg you just freed.
  3. Return to the first rope and tidy it.

If you have spent several moves and the count is higher than where you started, undo back to your lowest count and try a different first move. In games with no timer and no lives, that costs nothing.

Questions

What are the rules of a rope untangle game?

Each rope joins two pegs. You move one rope end at a time to an empty peg, and the level is solved when no rope crosses another. Some games add timers, lives or move limits on top; the core rule stays the same.

Does every rope puzzle have a solution?

It depends on how the game builds its levels. Games that start each level from a solved board and tangle it with legal moves always have an answer. Boards made by scattering ropes at random can, in principle, be unsolvable with the pegs available.

What should I do when I'm stuck on a rope puzzle?

Undo back to the point where you had the fewest crossings, find the rope with the most crossings, and look for a move that frees a peg for it, even if that move adds a crossing for now.

Doing this in Unskein

In Unskein, a red pin marks every crossing and the counter shows how many are left. Holding an end over a peg shows hollow pins for the crossings that move would create. Some knots are built so that only a move that looks like a step back gets you out. From level 20, raised pegs add a second layer: a rope between two raised pegs never crosses the ropes on the linen, which makes them the natural home for long ropes. A hint shows the next move, and a free rewind returns you to the solving path.
