Number matching game rules: how to play, step by step

Number matching game rules in plain words: which pairs you can strike, how pairs connect across lines and gaps, what an add does, and how a board ends.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

In a number matching game you clear a grid of digits by striking out pairs. Two numbers make a pair if they are equal (6 and 6) or if they add up to ten (2 and 8). The two numbers must also be connected: next to each other in a row, above each other in a column, on a diagonal, or across the end of one line and the start of the next, with only struck-out numbers allowed between them. When no pairs are left, you copy the remaining numbers to the bottom and keep going. You win when the grid is empty.

How to play: the two rules for a pair

Rule 1: the numbers match. Either they are the same digit, or they sum to ten. That gives this short list of partners:

DigitStrikes out with
11 or 9
22 or 8
33 or 7
44 or 6
55 only
66 or 4
77 or 3
88 or 2
99 or 1

There is no zero, and 5 is the odd one out: 5 + 5 is ten and also a matching pair, so a 5 can only ever go with another 5.

Rule 2: the numbers are connected. Walk from one number toward the other in a straight line. If every cell you pass on the way is already struck out, they are connected. That works in four directions:

  • Along the row, including the jump from the last number of one row to the first number of the next, as if you were reading.
  • Down the column.
  • Along a diagonal, in either direction. Diagonals stop at the edge of the grid; they never wrap.

Many apps play with all four. The original pencil game uses only rows (with the line jump) and columns.

A worked example

This is the traditional starting grid: the numbers 1 to 19 written in a row without the 10, digit by digit, nine to a line.

C1C2C3C4C5C6C7C8C9
R1123456789
R2111213141
R3516171819

Some pairs you could take at the start:

  • R2C1 and R2C2: two 1s side by side.
  • R1C9 and R2C9: 9 above 1, a column pair that makes ten.
  • R1C9 and R2C1: 9 at the end of row 1 and 1 at the start of row 2. They connect across the line end.
  • R3C8 and R3C9: 1 and 9, side by side.
  • R1C7 and R2C6: 7 and 3 on a diagonal. Legal with diagonals on, not in the paper version.

Now play three moves:

  1. Strike out the two 1s in R2C1 and R2C2.
  2. Look at the 9 in R1C9. Reading on from it, the next two numbers are struck out, so the first open number is the 1 in R2C3. 9 and 1 make ten across the line end and across the gap: strike them both.
  3. Strike out the 7 in R1C7 and the 3 in R2C6 along the diagonal.

Each strike opens new connections. That is the heart of the game: the pair you take now decides which pairs exist later.

When you run out of pairs: the add

When no legal pair is left, you add: write every number that is still open again, in the same order, starting right after the last number on the grid. On paper you simply keep writing in the same nine columns. The copy gives the board fresh neighbors and new pairs.

Most apps limit adds per board, often to just a few. A board is lost when there are no pairs and no adds left.

Clearing rows and ending the board

When every number in a row is struck out, the row disappears and the rows below move up, which can line up new column and diagonal pairs. Many apps give extra points for clearing rows close together. The board is won when every number is gone.

Common mistakes when learning

  • Forgetting the line jump. The last number of a row and the first of the next are neighbors.
  • Treating struck numbers as walls. They are gaps; look straight through them.
  • Pairing across an open number. If an unstruck number sits between two cells, they are not connected, even if they match.
  • Adding too early. An add copies every open number. Clear what you can first, so the copy is shorter.

Questions

How do you play the number game where you pair numbers that make 10?

Strike out two numbers that are equal or add up to ten and are connected in a row, column or diagonal, or across a line end, with only struck numbers between them. When no pairs remain, write the open numbers again at the bottom. Clear every number to win.

Can you match numbers diagonally?

In most phone versions, yes. In the traditional pencil game, no: only rows and columns count. Check which rule your game uses.

What happens when there are no more moves?

You use an add, which copies every open number to the end of the grid. If you have no adds left and no pairs, the board is over.

Can a 5 pair with anything other than a 5?

No. The only number that makes ten with 5 is another 5, so 5s always pair with each other.

Playing it in Tally Ten

Tally Ten uses these rules with diagonals on, and a Paper rules setting turns diagonals off for the original game. Before you play an add, it previews how many new pairs the copy will create. On Easy and Medium boards, a tick shows while the board can still be cleared, and a red-pen note names the move that lost it.
