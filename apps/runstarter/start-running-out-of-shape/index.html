How to start running when you're out of shape

How to start running when you're out of shape or haven't exercised in years: when to see a doctor first, walking before running, and a slow first week.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

To start running when you're out of shape, begin by walking, then add very short runs of about a minute with walking breaks in between, three times a week. Run slowly enough to talk in full sentences. Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition, chest pain, dizziness or another health condition. Most of the work in the first weeks is getting your legs used to the impact, so going slower than you think is the plan, not a weakness.

This page is general information about starting to exercise, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, take medication for your heart or blood pressure, are pregnant, or are unsure whether running is safe for you, talk to a doctor first.

Before your first run: when to see a doctor first

Most adults can start walking and gentle jogging without a check-up. The PAR-Q+, a widely used screening questionnaire, suggests talking to a doctor or a qualified exercise professional before becoming much more active if, for example:

  • a doctor has told you that you have a heart condition or high blood pressure
  • you feel chest pain at rest, during daily activities or when you are active
  • you lose balance because of dizziness, or have lost consciousness, in the last 12 months
  • you have another chronic medical condition, such as diabetes, or take prescribed medication for one
  • a bone, joint or soft-tissue problem could be made worse by being more active
  • a doctor has said you should only do medically supervised activity

A "yes" does not mean you can't run. It means getting advice on how to start safely. The official questionnaire is published at https://eparmedx.com.

Step 1: walk first if you need to

If a 30-minute walk at a brisk pace leaves you very out of breath or sore the next day, start with walking for a few weeks. A simple walking base:

WeekEach session, three times a week
14 times: brisk walk 3 min, easy walk 2 min
24 times: brisk walk 4 min, easy walk 2 min
33 times: brisk walk 6 min, easy walk 2 min
4Twice: brisk walk 10 min, easy walk 2 min
5Brisk walk 15 min, easy 2 min, brisk 10 min
6Brisk walk 30 min

Add a five-minute easy walk before and after each session. "Brisk" means walking like you're late for a bus while still able to talk. Once week 6 feels comfortable, you are ready for short runs.

Step 2: run one minute at a time

Your first running week should look like this: a five-minute walk, then one minute of slow jogging and a minute and a half of walking, eight times, then a five-minute walk. That is 8 minutes of running in a 30-minute session. Each week adds a little. The full nine weeks are in the beginner running plan.

Step 3: use the talk test

Run at a pace where you can still say a whole sentence. The CDC describes moderate effort as being able to talk but not sing, and vigorous effort as not being able to say more than a few words without pausing for breath. For a new runner, the running intervals should feel closer to moderate. If you can't finish a sentence, slow down. If you're already jogging as slowly as you can, walk.

This feels too slow to many beginners. It isn't. A slow jog lets you finish the session, and finishing sessions is what builds fitness.

What to expect in the first weeks

  • Heavy legs and breathlessness in the first runs are normal. They ease over the first two or three weeks.
  • Mild muscle soreness a day or two after a run is common at the start. It should fade within a few days.
  • Some sessions feel harder than others for no clear reason: sleep, heat, stress and food all play a part.

Stop and walk, and get medical advice, if you feel chest pain or pressure, dizziness, faintness, unusual shortness of breath, or a sharp or worsening pain in a joint or bone. Pain that makes you limp is a reason to stop running until a professional has looked at it.

Starting at a higher body weight

Running at any size works the same way: start with walking, keep runs short and slow, and build gradually. A few things make it more comfortable:

  • Choose softer, even ground where you can, such as a park path or a running track.
  • Get shoes that feel comfortable when you jog in the shop, and replace them when they are clearly worn.
  • Wear clothing that doesn't rub, such as close-fitting shorts or leggings, and fix any chafing spot before the next run.
  • Stay longer in the walking plan if your knees or feet complain. There is no deadline.

If you hate running

Many people who "hate running" have only tried running too fast. Try a session at walking-plus pace with music or a podcast, on a route you like, with a friend if possible. Set a goal you can see, such as a local 5K in three months. Track sessions, not speed.

Common mistakes

  • Starting too fast, then stopping after a few minutes.
  • Running on consecutive days in the first weeks. Rest days are when your body adapts.
  • Jumping ahead because a week felt easy. The step after an easy week is often the hard one.
  • Quitting after a missed week. Go back one week and carry on.

Questions

How do I start running if I'm out of shape?

Walk first if 30 minutes of brisk walking is hard, then start with one-minute runs and walking breaks three times a week. Keep the runs slow enough to talk.

Should I see a doctor before I start running?

Check with a doctor first if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, chest pain, dizziness, a chronic condition, or a joint problem that activity could make worse. The PAR-Q+ questionnaire is a quick way to check.

How slow should a beginner run?

Slow enough to speak a full sentence. If you can only say a few words, slow down or walk until your breathing settles.

Is it normal to be sore after my first run?

Mild muscle soreness for a day or two is common. Sharp pain, pain that makes you limp, chest pain or dizziness are reasons to stop and get medical advice.
