How to solve sudoku step by step, for beginners

How to solve sudoku for beginners, step by step: the rules, where to start, and the two techniques that finish easy puzzles, on a real example grid.

Updated 26 Nov 20266 min read

To solve a sudoku, fill the empty cells so that every row, every column and every 3×3 box holds the digits 1 to 9 exactly once. You never need arithmetic and you never need to guess: you place a digit only when the rest of the grid leaves it no other home. Start with the digit that appears most often, scan each box for the one cell where it can go, then look at single cells for the one digit they can hold. Those two moves finish most easy puzzles.

Sudoku rules for beginners

A few words make every explanation easier:

  • Cell: one of the 81 squares.
  • Row runs across, column runs down, box is one of the nine 3×3 blocks with the thicker border.
  • Givens: the digits printed at the start. They are always correct.
  • Candidates (or pencil marks): the digits a cell could still hold.

The only rule is that no digit repeats in a row, column or box. A well-made puzzle has exactly one solution, which is why logic alone can always reach it.

Step 1: scan a box for one digit

Count the digits first. The 9 appears seven times, more than any other, so only two 9s are missing: one in box 1 and one in box 5. A digit with many copies is the easiest to place because those copies block so much of the grid.

The 9 in box 1. Row 1 already has a 9 (in C6) and so does row 3 (in C9), so the 9 for box 1 must sit in row 2. Row 2 of box 1 has two empty cells, R2C1 and R2C2. Column 1 already has a 9 in R5C1, which rules out R2C1. The 9 goes in R2C2.

The 9 in box 5. Row 4 has a 9 in C7 and row 5 has one in C1, so box 5's 9 sits in row 6. Of row 6's empty cells in that box, R6C5 shares column 5 with the 9 in R8C5. The 9 goes in R6C4.

This is called cross-hatching: you draw imaginary lines out from each copy of a digit, and the one cell in the box that no line crosses is the answer. The technique it finds is the hidden single. The cell may have room for other digits, but it is the only place left for this one.

Try it once more with 8 in box 2. Row 3 has an 8 in C7, so row 3 is out. Column 6 has an 8 in R6C6, so R2C6 is out. That leaves R1C5 for the 8.

Step 2: check one cell for its only digit

Now turn it around and pick a cell. Take R1C3. Row 1 holds 2, 4, 7, 8 and 9. Column 3 holds 1, 5, 6, 8 and 9. Box 1 holds 1, 4, 6, 7 and the 9 you just placed. Put those together and the only digit missing from all three is 3, so R1C3 is 3.

The same check on R2C6 gives row 2 (1, 3, 6, 7, 9), column 6 (3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9) and box 2 (1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9). Only 2 is left, so R2C6 is 2. A cell with just one possible digit is a naked single.

Cells in crowded areas are the best ones to check: a cell whose row, column and box are already mostly full has very few options.

Step 3: work in a loop

  1. Pick the digit with the most copies on the grid and cross-hatch every box that still lacks it.
  2. Move to the next most common digit, and so on down to the rarest.
  3. When a placement lands, rescan its row, column and box straight away. A new digit often unlocks a neighbor.
  4. When a full pass over all nine digits finds nothing, check the fullest rows, columns and boxes cell by cell for naked singles.

Easy and medium puzzles usually fall to this loop alone. In the example above, the first pass already finds a hidden single in every box except box 6.

Step 4: add pencil marks when scanning dries up

On harder puzzles the loop stops. That is the moment for candidates: write in each empty cell every digit it could still hold, small, in the corners. Then two habits keep them useful:

  • When you place a digit, erase it from the candidates of every cell in the same row, column and box.
  • Fill candidates for a whole box or row at once, not cell by cell, so you do not skip one.

With complete candidates, any cell showing a single mark is a naked single, and patterns such as pairs start to show. The next steps are in the guide to sudoku techniques in order.

Common beginner mistakes

MistakeWhat to do instead
Writing a digit because it "looks likely"Place it only when you can say which row, column or box rules out every other option
Scanning only rowsCheck boxes too; most early placements come from boxes
Forgetting to rescan after a placementEvery new digit changes its row, column and box, so look there first
Leaving old pencil marks behindErase candidates the moment a digit is placed nearby
Starting with the rarest digitStart with the most common one; it blocks the most cells

Questions

Where should you start a sudoku?

Start with the digit that already appears most often and scan each box that is missing it. Crowded rows and boxes are also good starting points, because their empty cells have the fewest options.

Do you need to be good at math to play sudoku?

No. The digits are only symbols; you could play with nine letters or colors. Sudoku is a logic puzzle about where things can and cannot go.

What is the easiest way to solve sudoku?

Alternate between two moves: find the one cell in a box where a digit can go, and find the one digit a crowded cell can hold. Repeat after every placement. This solves most easy puzzles without pencil marks.

Is it okay to guess in sudoku?

You can, but you do not need to. A puzzle with one solution can always be solved by reasoning, and a guess that turns out wrong can spoil the grid many moves later without you noticing.
