Sudoku techniques in order, from pointing pairs to X-Wing

Sudoku techniques in order, explained with example grids: pointing pairs, box line reduction, hidden and naked pairs, X-Wing and Y-Wing.

Updated 26 Nov 20267 min read

Sudoku techniques are named patterns that let you remove candidates or place a digit without guessing. Learn them in order of difficulty: the two singles first, then pointing pairs and box line reduction, then naked and hidden pairs, triples and quads, and finally X-Wing and Y-Wing. Each one below comes with a small example grid, written as a table, that shows only the candidates that matter for that step.

All of these work on pencil marks. If you have not used candidates before, start with how to solve sudoku step by step, which covers the rules and the two singles.

Pointing pairs and box line reduction are together called locked candidates. The "usually needed for" column follows the four levels used in Sudoku Koan; other apps and newspapers label their levels differently.

Pointing pairs

Look at one box and one digit. If every cell in that box where the digit can go lies on the same row (or column), the digit must land on that line inside the box. So it cannot appear anywhere else on that line.

In this example, only the 5s that matter are shown. In box 1, the 5 can only go in R2C1 or R2C3. Both are in row 2, so the 5s pencilled in R2C5 and R2C8 can be erased.

If three cells in the box share the line, it is a pointing triple, and the logic is the same.

Box line reduction

This is the mirror image. Look at one row (or column) and one digit. If all its candidates for the digit fall inside a single box, the digit must go there on that row, so every other cell of that box loses it.

Here row 4 can only take a 3 in R4C4 or R4C6, both inside box 5. The 3s pencilled in R5C5 and R6C4 can go.

Naked pairs

If two cells in the same row, column or box hold exactly the same two candidates, say 4 and 6, those two digits are spoken for: one cell gets the 4 and the other the 6. Erase 4 and 6 from every other cell in that unit. A naked triple works the same way with three cells whose candidates, together, are only three digits (each cell may show just two of them).

Hidden pairs

A hidden pair is the same idea seen from the digits' side, and it is harder to spot because other candidates crowd the cells. Here is row 6 with all its candidates. R6C4 and R6C6 are already filled.

CellC1C2C3C4C5C6C7C8C9
Before2 4 81 4 7 82 453 4 8 962 31 2 73 8 9
After2 4 81 72 453 4 8 962 31 73 8 9

Scan the row digit by digit. The 1 appears only in C2 and C8. So does the 7. Two digits with the same two possible cells must fill those cells, so every other candidate there goes: R6C2 drops 4 and 8, R6C8 drops 2. Now they read as a naked pair.

The habit that finds hidden pairs: for each unit, count how many cells each digit can use. Any digit with exactly two cells is worth noting; two digits sharing the same two cells is the pattern.

X-Wing

An X-Wing uses one digit across two rows. If in row 2 the digit can only go in two cells, and in row 7 it can only go in two cells in the same two columns, the four cells form a rectangle. The digit must take opposite corners: either top left and bottom right, or top right and bottom left. Either way it fills both columns, so every other candidate for it in those two columns can be erased.

In this grid, the 4 can only go in C3 or C8 in both row 2 and row 7. The 4s in R4C3, R9C3 and R5C8 are removed. The 4 in R5C2 is in neither column, so it stays.

The same works with columns and rows swapped. The rows need not be next to each other, and the corners need not share a box. Extended to three rows and three columns, the pattern is called a Swordfish.

Y-Wing (XY-Wing)

A Y-Wing uses three cells that each have exactly two candidates. The middle cell, the pivot, sees both others. Here the pivot R5C5 holds 2 or 7. R5C1, in the same row, holds 2 or 9. R2C5, in the same column, holds 7 or 9.

If the pivot is 2, R5C1 must be 9. If the pivot is 7, R2C5 must be 9. One of the two ends is a 9 whichever way it goes, so any cell that sees both ends cannot be 9. R2C1 sees R2C5 along row 2 and R5C1 along column 1, so it loses its 9 and becomes a naked single: 3.

How to practice these techniques

  1. Learn one at a time. Play several puzzles where you look only for that pattern before adding the next.
  2. Keep complete candidates. Every pattern above depends on pencil marks being accurate; one missing candidate makes a false pattern.
  3. Always run the easy techniques first. After any elimination, check for new singles before hunting for an X-Wing.
  4. Say the reason aloud: "the 4 in row 2 can only go in columns 3 and 8". If you cannot say it, do not erase.

When a hard puzzle stops moving and you are not sure what to look for, use the checklist for when you are stuck at sudoku.

Questions

What order should I learn sudoku techniques in?

Hidden and naked singles, then pointing pairs and box line reduction, then naked and hidden pairs, triples and quads, then X-Wing and Y-Wing. Each step builds on the eliminations of the ones before it.

What is the difference between a naked pair and a hidden pair?

A naked pair is two cells that hold only the same two candidates. A hidden pair is two digits that can only go in the same two cells, even though those cells show other candidates too. Once you strip the extras, a hidden pair becomes a naked pair.

What is an X-Wing in sudoku?

A rectangle of four cells for one digit: two rows where the digit has exactly two places, lined up in the same two columns. The digit must take opposite corners, so it can be removed from the rest of both columns.

Do I need advanced techniques to solve hard sudoku?

For most hard newspaper and app puzzles, locked candidates and pairs are enough. Expert grids may need X-Wing or Y-Wing. Only the hardest "extreme" puzzles go beyond these, into chains and similar patterns.
