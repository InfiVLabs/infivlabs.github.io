How to stop alcohol cravings when they hit

How to stop alcohol cravings in the moment: how long an urge lasts, urge surfing step by step, playing the tape forward, and when to get medical help.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

You can't switch a craving off, but you can outlast it. Ireland's health service (HSE) says a craving usually peaks and eases within 20 to 30 minutes, and the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) describes urges to drink as short-lived, predictable and controllable. The aim in the moment is to get through the next half hour without a drink. The tools below are built for exactly that.

If cravings come with shaking, sweating, nausea or anxiety when you haven't had a drink, that may be withdrawal, not just an urge. Stopping heavy drinking suddenly can be dangerous. Talk to a doctor, and call 911 (999 in the UK) or go to an emergency room for hallucinations, severe shaking or a seizure.

How long do alcohol cravings last?

A single craving usually rises, peaks and fades within 20 to 30 minutes, though that varies from person to person. In the first week after stopping, cravings can be strong and frequent; the HSE says these severe cravings usually ease after about 7 days. They can keep coming back for much longer, but they tend to get weaker and easier to handle over time. The NIAAA notes that with practice, urges lose strength.

Every craving you ride out without drinking is practice, and proof for next time that the urge ends on its own.

Urge surfing, step by step

Urge surfing is a technique from mindfulness-based relapse prevention, which grew out of psychologist Alan Marlatt's work on relapse. Instead of fighting the urge, you watch it like a wave: it builds, crests and breaks. The NIAAA's advice puts it the same way: accept the urge as temporary and ride it out, and it will soon crest like an ocean wave and pass.

  1. Notice and name it. Say to yourself, "I'm having an urge to drink." Naming it helps you watch the urge instead of obeying it.
  2. Find it in your body. Where is it? A tight chest, a dry mouth, restless hands? Describe it as if to someone else.
  3. Rate it from 0 to 10. Write the number down or say it out loud.
  4. Breathe and watch. Slow breaths, longer out than in. Don't argue with the urge or give in to it. Just observe it.
  5. Rate it again every few minutes. You will often see the number rise, flatten, then fall.
  6. Note when it has passed. Write the time. Next time you have proof it ends.

A ten-minute timer helps. You are not promising never to drink, only to wait ten minutes. Often that is enough; if not, set another ten.

Play the tape forward

When the urge says "one drink will help", run the film past the first sip:

  • The next hour: what happens after the first drink? Is it really one?
  • Tomorrow morning: how do you feel when you wake up? What do you have to explain or redo?
  • A week from now: what does this choice add up to?

Answer honestly, and in detail. The craving shows you the first five minutes; the tape shows you the rest.

Six more ways to get through a craving

These come from the NIAAA's Rethinking Drinking toolkit and the HSE's "4 Ds":

ToolWhat to do
DelayTell yourself you'll decide in 20 minutes, and set a timer.
DistractWalk around the block, shower, call someone, cook, play a game.
Deep breathsTake 20 slow, deep breaths.
Drink something elseSip a glass of cold water or juice slowly.
Remind yourselfRead your reasons for stopping, kept on a card or in your phone.
Talk it throughCall or text someone you trust, and say you are having an urge.

Two more from Rethinking Drinking work before the craving starts: keep little or no alcohol at home, and leave a tempting situation quickly, with your exit planned in advance.

Know your triggers

Cravings follow patterns. The NIAAA suggests tracking your urges for a couple of weeks: the time, the place, who you were with and how you felt. Common triggers include:

  • Times of day, such as 6 pm after work or Friday evening
  • Places, such as a particular bar, the couch, or a grocery store aisle
  • People, such as drinking friends or a stressful family member
  • Feelings, such as boredom, loneliness, anger, celebration or tiredness
  • Body states, such as hunger and poor sleep, which make cravings feel stronger

Once you know your top two or three, plan around them: a different route home, food at 5:30 pm, a call booked for Friday evening.

When cravings are too strong to manage alone

If you keep giving in despite trying these tools, or you have been through a few weeks of strategies without progress, talk to a doctor. The NIAAA says to consult a healthcare professional in that case. There are prescription medicines for alcohol use disorder, and the NIAAA notes that one of them, naltrexone, can reduce craving. Talk therapy and support groups help many people. A doctor can also check whether stopping is safe for you.

Questions

How do you stop alcohol cravings fast?

Delay for 20 minutes with a timer, take 20 slow breaths, drink a glass of cold water and do something with your hands or feet. Most cravings peak and ease within 20 to 30 minutes, so the goal is to fill that time.

How long do alcohol cravings last?

A single craving usually lasts 20 to 30 minutes. The strongest cravings after stopping usually ease after about the first week, and they tend to get weaker over the following weeks and months.

What is urge surfing?

Urge surfing means noticing a craving, observing how it feels in your body and letting it rise and fall like a wave without acting on it. It comes from mindfulness-based relapse prevention.

What does playing the tape forward mean?

It means imagining the whole sequence after the first drink: the next hour, the next morning and the next week, instead of only the first few minutes the craving promises.
