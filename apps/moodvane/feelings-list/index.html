List of feelings and emotions: 120 words to name how you feel

A list of feelings and emotions for adults, grouped from mild to strong, plus how to name an emotion and how to draw your own feelings wheel.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

Many of us describe our feelings with a handful of words: good, bad, fine, stressed, tired. The list of feelings below gives you 120 more precise ones, grouped into eight families and arranged from mild to strong, so you can find the word that fits instead of the one that's nearest. Naming a feeling precisely is a good first step to understanding what caused it.

That is 120 words. Some sit in more than one family; "overwhelmed" can be fear, sadness and exhaustion at once. Pick the one that feels closest, or use two.

How to name an emotion in four steps

  1. Start with the body. Where do you feel it? Tight chest, hot face, heavy limbs, a knot in your stomach, restless legs.
  2. Pick the family. Pleasant or unpleasant? High energy or low? That narrows it to one or two rows of the table.
  3. Find the closest word. Read across the row from mild to strong. "Angry" might really be "frustrated" or "resentful", which point at different things.
  4. Add the because. "I feel resentful because I did the dishes again." The reason usually tells you what you need.

Why naming a feeling helps

In a brain-imaging study at UCLA (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007), people who put a word to the emotion in a picture showed less activity in the amygdala, an area involved in emotional reactions, than when they did other tasks with the same pictures. Psychologists call this affect labeling. It doesn't make a feeling go away, but naming it is a simple first step to stepping back from it.

Pleasant or unpleasant, high or low energy

A useful way to sort feelings is on two scales at once: how pleasant they are and how much energy they carry. Psychologist James Russell described this two-dimensional map of emotions in 1980, and many mood tools use a version of it.

Low energyHigh energy
Pleasantcalm, content, relaxed, peaceful, tenderexcited, joyful, playful, proud, energetic
Unpleasantsad, tired, bored, lonely, numbanxious, angry, panicked, frustrated, tense

If you can't find a word, start here. Just knowing "unpleasant and high energy" tells you that slowing your breathing or moving your body might help more than a nap.

Draw your own feelings wheel

A feelings wheel puts emotion words in a circle so you can move from broad to specific. You can make your own in five minutes:

  1. Draw three circles, one inside the other, like a target.
  2. Split the middle circle into eight slices, one for each family in the table above: happy, calm, connected, sad, angry, afraid, ashamed, drained.
  3. Put pleasant families next to each other, and unpleasant ones next to each other, so neighbors are related.
  4. In the middle ring, write two moderate words per slice. In the outer ring, write two or three strong or specific ones.
  5. Color each slice, and keep the wheel where you journal.

When you're not sure what you feel, start in the center and move outwards. Making your own means the words are ones you'd actually use.

Common mix-ups

  • "Stressed" often means anxious (worried about what's coming) or overwhelmed (too much at once). They need different fixes.
  • "Angry" often sits on top of hurt or embarrassment.
  • "Tired" can be sleepy, bored, sad or burnt out.
  • "Fine" is not a feeling. Try again with the table.

Questions

What are the main types of emotions?

There is no single agreed list, but most lists include happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise and disgust. For everyday use, the eight families above add calm, connection, shame and exhaustion, which people feel often.

How do I identify what I'm feeling?

Notice where you feel it in your body, decide whether it's pleasant or unpleasant and high or low energy, then pick the closest word from a feelings list. Adding "because" often shows you the cause.

What is a feelings wheel?

A circle of emotion words arranged from broad in the middle to specific at the edge. It helps you move from a vague feeling like "bad" to a precise word like "left out".

Why is it hard to name emotions?

Many people never learned many feeling words, and strong feelings make thinking harder. A written list helps because you only need to recognize the word, not come up with it.
