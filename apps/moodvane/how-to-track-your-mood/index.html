How to track your mood and find what lifts your days

How to track your mood in under a minute a day: pick a scale, log what you did, then compare your better days with the rest. With a worked example.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

To track your mood, rate how you feel on a simple scale once a day, at about the same time, and note two or three things you did that day. After three or four weeks, compare the days you did each thing with the days you didn't. That comparison, not the daily score, is what tells you what affects your mood.

Here is how to set it up so it takes less than a minute a day and still tells you something useful.

Step 1: Pick a mood scale you'll actually use

A five-point scale works well for a daily log:

ScoreLabelWhat it means
5GreatEnergetic, happy, at ease
4GoodPleasant and steady
3OkayNeutral, mixed or flat
2LowTired, irritable, sad or worried
1Very lowA hard day

A 1 to 10 mood scale gives more detail, but it is easy to drift into using the same few middle numbers every day, which hides the differences. If you choose 10 points, write a one-line description for 2, 5 and 8 so your scores mean the same thing in March as they did in January.

Step 2: Decide when to log

Pick one fixed moment, ideally tied to something you already do: after dinner, when you plug your phone in, before bed. Score the day as a whole, not how you feel right that minute.

Logging more than once a day is fine if your mood swings a lot between morning and evening. Just keep the evening entry as the one you compare.

Step 3: Log what you did, not just how you felt

A mood score on its own makes a nice chart and very few insights. The useful part is context. Each day, tick or write a few things from a short list:

  • Sleep: good, okay or bad
  • Movement: workout, walk, none
  • People: friends, family, alone time, a date
  • Work or study: productive, deadline, day off
  • Other: outdoors, reading, gaming, cooking, alcohol, sick

Keep the list to about 15 to 25 items, so ticking them takes seconds. Add a new one when you notice something that seems to matter, like "skipped lunch" or "long commute".

Step 4: Add one line if you want

A mood journal does not need paragraphs. One sentence is plenty: what happened, or the precise feeling. "Snapped at Sam, mostly tired" is more useful in a month than a page written once and never again. If you struggle to find the right word, a list of feelings and emotions helps.

Step 5: Compare after three or four weeks

Once you have about 20 to 30 days, take one activity at a time:

  1. Average your mood on the days with it.
  2. Average your mood on the days without it.
  3. Note how many days are on each side.

A worked example

Say you logged 30 days and walked on 12 of them:

DaysAverage mood
With a walk123.8
Without a walk183.1
Difference+0.7

That's a pattern worth watching: your walking days averaged 0.7 points higher. But it is not proof that walking lifts your mood. Maybe you walk more on days off or in good weather. Keep tracking and see if it holds over the next month, and check the other side too: do bad-sleep days drag the average down?

A difference based on two or three days means little. Wait until each side has at least five days before you take it seriously.

What affects your mood: patterns worth checking

Everyone is different, so test these against your own entries after a few months:

  • Sleep the night before: compare good-sleep days with the rest.
  • The day after: a late night, a big event or drinking can show up the next day rather than on the day itself.
  • Weekdays: some people see a shape, such as a low Monday and a lift on Friday.
  • Seeing people: company lifts some people and drains others. Your data will tell you which you are.
  • Combinations: a walk with a friend may matter more than either alone.

Common mistakes when tracking your mood

  • Only logging bad days. Then every day looks bad. Log the boring ones too.
  • Changing the scale. If you switch from five points to ten, start a fresh comparison.
  • Expecting answers in a week. You need a few weeks for patterns to show.
  • Reading cause into coincidence. Treat patterns as questions to test.
  • Turning it into homework. If it takes more than a minute, cut the list down.

Questions

How do I start a mood journal?

Choose a five-point scale, a fixed time each day and a short list of activities to tick. Log for 30 days before judging what you see. A paper notebook or a printable 2027 mood calendar works as well as an app.

What should I write in a mood journal?

Your score, what you did that day, and one sentence about what happened or how you felt. Specific beats long: "anxious before the meeting, fine after" is more useful than a page.

How long should you track your mood before you see patterns?

Give it three to four weeks. With fewer than five days on each side of a comparison, differences are mostly noise.

Is a mood tracker useful for therapy?

It can be. A month of entries on one page is often easier to talk through at an appointment than memory alone. It's a conversation starter for you and your therapist, not a diagnosis.
