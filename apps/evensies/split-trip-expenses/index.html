How to split trip expenses with friends

Split trip expenses fairly: work out each person's balance, then who owes who in the fewest payments, shown with a real four-friend trip and table.

Updated 26 Nov 20265 min read

To split trip expenses, list every cost with who paid it and who shared it, then work out one number per person: what they paid minus their fair share. People with a plus are owed money, people with a minus owe it, and the minus side pays the plus side until everyone is at zero. Done carefully, a group of four never needs more than three payments.

Splitting trip expenses: four friends, one weekend

Asha, Ben, Chen and Dana rent a cabin for a weekend. Five things cost money, and four different people paid for them:

Notice the kayaks. A fair split follows who actually used something, so Chen pays nothing toward them. Most arguments about trip money start with an even split of something that wasn't shared evenly.

Step 1: each person's fair share

Divide each expense among the people who shared it, then add up each person's column.

Step 3: settle in the fewest payments

You could pay back every expense on its own. That means Ben, Chen and Dana each paying Asha for the cabin, everyone paying Ben for groceries, and so on. Even after netting each pair of people against each other, this trip still needs six payments:

Pair-by-pair paymentsAmount
Ben → Asha62.50
Chen → Asha130.00
Dana → Asha130.00
Chen → Ben67.50
Dana → Ben67.50
Dana → Chen30.00

Using the balances instead gets the same result with half the transfers. The simple method: the person who owes the most pays the person who is owed the most, as much as one of them needs. Repeat until everyone is at zero.

  1. Dana owes 227.50, Asha is owed 322.50. Dana pays Asha $227.50. Asha is still owed 95.00.
  2. Chen owes 167.50, Asha is owed 95.00. Chen pays Asha $95.00. Chen still owes 72.50.
  3. Chen owes 72.50, Ben is owed 72.50. Chen pays Ben $72.50. Everyone is at zero.

Three payments, and every person ends up exactly where the pair-by-pair version would have left them. Nobody receives money only to pass it on.

The math behind the fewest payments

With n people who aren't already even, you never need more than n − 1 payments: each payment brings at least one person to zero, and the last payment clears the final two. The method above always achieves that.

Sometimes you can do better. If some people's balances cancel out among themselves, each such group can settle separately and saves a payment. Take five friends with balances of +6, +4, −3, −3 and −4. The largest-pays-largest method gives four payments. But −4 and +4 cancel, and −3, −3 and +6 cancel, so three payments are enough: the −4 pays the +4, and both −3s pay the +6. Finding those cancelling groups is fiddly by hand once a group gets past five or six people, which is the main reason to let a calculator or app do it.

Common mistakes

  • Splitting everything equally by habit. The kayaks, the bar tab, the one person who skipped dinner. Mark who shared each cost when you add it, not at the end.
  • Forgetting the small stuff. A $12 parking fee feels too small to log, but five of them is $60 that one person quietly carried.
  • Rounding each line in your head. $100 split three ways is 33.34, 33.33 and 33.33. Decide once who takes the extra cent (usually someone who didn't pay) and the totals stay exact.
  • Paying back as you go. Paying for each expense separately during the trip creates far more transfers than one round at the end.
  • Mixing currencies without a rate. On a trip abroad, agree the exchange rate for each foreign expense and convert to one currency before working out balances.

What to do when someone disputes a number

Go back to the two checks: shares add up to the total spent, and balances add up to zero. If both hold, the disagreement is about an expense, not the math: who shared it, or whether it was a group cost at all. Fix that line and recalculate. Sharing the full expense list before anyone pays prevents most of these conversations.

Questions

How do you split expenses between 3 people?

Add up what each of the three paid and divide every shared cost by three (or by however many people shared it). Each person's balance is what they paid minus their share. With three people, at most two payments make everyone even: whoever owes pays whoever is owed.

Who owes who when several people paid?

Work out each person's balance (paid minus fair share) across all expenses, not expense by expense. Anyone with a negative balance owes; anyone with a positive balance is owed. Then match the largest debt with the largest credit and repeat. That never takes more than three payments for four people, or four for five.

What is "simplify debts"?

It means replacing all the pair-by-pair debts in a group with the shortest list of payments that leaves everyone with the same final result. In the example above, six pair-by-pair payments became three.
