OMAD (one meal a day): how it works and how it compares with 16:8

OMAD, one meal a day, explained: how the 23:1 fast works, how it compares with 16:8, what the small studies found, and who should not try it.

Updated 26 Nov 20264 min read

OMAD means eating one meal a day: you fast for about 23 hours and eat all your food in a window of about an hour, usually in the evening. It is the strictest of the daily intermittent fasting schedules. It is simple to describe and hard to do well, because one meal has to carry a whole day's protein, fibre, vitamins and minerals, and research on it in people is limited to a few small, short trials.

OMAD is an extended daily fast. This page is not medical advice. Talk to a doctor before trying it, and do not try it if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, under 18, have diabetes, take medicine that lowers blood sugar, or have had an eating disorder.

What an OMAD day looks like

TimeWhat happens
7:00 AMWake up. Water, black coffee or plain tea
Through the dayWater, sparkling water, black coffee, unsweetened tea
6:00 PMThe one meal, eaten over about an hour
7:00 PMThe fast begins again, until 6 PM the next day

Some people call a two-meal version "OMAD" too, or eat over two hours (22:2). Strictly, OMAD is a single meal inside about an hour.

OMAD vs 16:8

16:8OMAD
Fasting each day16 hoursabout 23 hours
Eating window8 hoursabout 1 hour
MealsUsually two, maybe a snackOne
Getting enough nutrientsEasier: two meals to spread food acrossHarder: everything has to fit one plate
Time without food16 hours, much of it asleepAbout 23 hours, most of it awake
Fits around family and social mealsUsually, if dinner is in the windowOnly if your one meal is the shared one
Research in peopleMore studies of time-restricted eatingFew, small and short

For most people 16:8 is the more practical choice, and OMAD is something some people do on certain days rather than every day. If you are curious about OMAD, it is sensible to have kept 16:8 and then 18:6 or 20:4 comfortably first. The intermittent fasting schedule guide covers those plans.

What the research says

There is not much of it. Two small studies come up most often:

  • A 2007 US Department of Agriculture trial had healthy, normal-weight, middle-aged adults eat all their usual calories either in three meals a day or in one meal a day, for 8 weeks each. On one meal a day, people were significantly hungrier, and their blood pressure and their total, LDL and HDL cholesterol went up.
  • An 11-day trial in 11 young, lean people, described by a nutrition lecturer at the University of Nottingham, found bigger losses of lean mass and bone density on one meal a day.

The same nutrition expert's summary: it is hard to meet your nutritional needs in a single meal, very little is known about long-term effects, and she would not recommend it for children, pregnant or breastfeeding people, or anyone at risk of an eating disorder.

That does not mean OMAD harms everyone, only that the evidence is thin and the known short-term effects are not all good.

Who should not try OMAD

  • Anyone pregnant or breastfeeding
  • Children and teenagers under 18
  • People with type 1 diabetes, or type 2 diabetes on insulin or other medicine that lowers blood sugar
  • Anyone with a current or past eating disorder
  • People who are underweight or malnourished, or have low blood sugar episodes
  • People with long-term health conditions, or who take medicine that must be taken with food

If any of these apply, talk to your doctor before any fasting plan, not just OMAD.

If you do try it

  1. Build the meal on purpose. Protein (lentils, beans, eggs, fish, meat or tofu), plenty of vegetables, a wholegrain starch, fruit, and a dairy food or calcium-rich alternative.
  2. Don't skip fluids. Drink water through the day.
  3. Try it on one or two days a week first, on top of a 16:8 routine, rather than every day.
  4. Stop if you feel unwell. Dizziness, fainting, shakiness, palpitations or feeling faint are reasons to eat now.
  5. Keep medicines on schedule. If a medicine needs food, that comes first; ask your pharmacist.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the one meal as a free-for-all. A very large, rich meal after 23 hours often leads to discomfort and doesn't fix missing nutrients.
  • Drinking calories through the day: milky coffee, juice or sweet drinks end the fast (see what breaks a fast).
  • Going straight from three meals to OMAD. Step down through shorter fasts first.
  • Ignoring a pattern of bingeing or anxiety about food. That is a sign to stop and get support.

Questions

What is OMAD?

OMAD stands for one meal a day. You eat once, within about an hour, and fast for the other 23 hours, drinking only water and other calorie-free drinks.

Is OMAD better than 16:8?

There is no good evidence that it is. 16:8 is easier to keep, makes it simpler to eat enough nutrients, and has been studied more. OMAD is much stricter, and small studies found more hunger and higher blood pressure and cholesterol on one meal a day.

Can I drink coffee on OMAD?

Black coffee without sugar or milk is generally considered fine during the fasting hours. Milk, sugar, syrups and creamers add calories and end the fast.

How long is the eating window on OMAD?

About one hour, which makes it a 23:1 schedule. A two-hour window (22:2) is sometimes counted as OMAD as well.
